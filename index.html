Miniature pressure-relief valve

A passive valve that vents a superpressure balloon above a set pressure and reseals as it falls.

Problem
At 1–6.5 kPa the valve has only a few newtons to work with, and it must stay light and seal in the cold.
My contribution
Sole mechanical engineer: concepts, CAD, SLA-printed prototypes, test method and software, ambient and cold testing, analysis and redesign.
Outcome
A diaphragm architecture that separates pressure sensing from sealing, with cracking pressure set by spring preload.
Status
Development prototype, characterised in air at ambient and cold temperatures; not yet qualified.

Engineering detail The design changeWhat testing showedStill open

The design change

The first concept was direct-acting: the same area collected the pressure and formed the seal, so a few newtons were spread over a large seal face and sealing depended on finish and lubricant. The diaphragm design separates the two: a Kapton diaphragm collects pressure over about 600 mm², and the plunger concentrates that force on a 3.175 mm steel ball on a machined PTFE seat.

What testing showed

Cracking pressure followed preload linearly across six settings at 28 °C. Replacing the Mylar diaphragm with Kapton reduced the fitted offset force from about 0.44 N to 0.084 N; the remainder may partly be a preload or assembly-reference error.

02Noove, Project Picoballoon2025–26

Balloon Management System

An automated pneumatic platform for balloon preparation and valve testing.

Problem
Each superpressure balloon is pre-stretched at its simulated float pressure before flight. By hand, one stretch meant hours of watching a gauge and correcting a pump, and valve testing needed the same repeatable pressure and flow control.
My contribution
Requirements, component selection, pneumatic integration, Python/PyQt control and logging software, the firmware interface, testing and iteration. The ESP32 controller PCB and the initial firmware were designed by the project’s electronics engineer.
Outcome
Stretch sequences run unattended, from fast fill to automatic deflation, with safety interlocks.
Status
Operational: used for balloon preparation and as the test rig for the pressure-relief valve (01).
Balloons stretched on the platform
50+
Balloon volumes handled
80–1100L
Engineering detail Pneumatic layoutControl strategyTwo configurationsWhat I would change

Pneumatic layout

Two 3/2 solenoid valves swap the pump’s suction and discharge between ambient and the balloon line, so one fixed-direction diaphragm pump both inflates and deflates. I chose a multi-gas Bronkhorst flow meter over a helium-specific one: it covers air, argon and nitrogen, and the helium quantity is set separately by free-lift weighing.

Control strategy

A balloon is a strongly nonlinear load. Early in inflation it takes a lot of gas for almost no pressure rise; near its inflated shape, pressure climbs steeply. One pump command cannot serve both, so the sequence runs in four stages: open-loop fast fill to a switch pressure, an approach that regulates flow rather than pressure, a dead-band pressure hold, and automatic deflation that ends on pressure.

Interlocks stop the test on overpressure, a lost or stale pressure reading, a lost controller link, or pressure failing to rise while the pump runs. It took several software iterations before the sequence ran unattended.

Two configurations, measured separately

Balloon stretching

On-board differential-pressure sensor with dead-band regulation during the hold. In a bench cross-check, this sensor read within ±2 Pa of a Pressurewell reference: a sensor comparison, not the hold accuracy.

Valve characterisation

The platform drives the pump and valves and measures flow, while an independent MicroStep Pressurewell supplies the pressure reference, logged over TCP at about 9 Hz. This keeps the valve under test out of the control loop.

What I would change

The dead-band hold is adequate for stretching, but a PI controller would give smoother corrections and tighter regulation during the hold.

03University of Perugia, AMS-02 Layer 0 upgrade2022–23

AMS-02 Layer 0 integration tooling

Ground fixtures for bonding fragile silicon-sensor ladders onto carbon-composite panels.

Problem
Each silicon-sensor ladder had to land within about ±100 µm, 200 µm from its neighbour. The tooling had to keep the ladder’s datum through handling and transfer, position it relative to the carbon-composite panel, and control the ~100 µm adhesive layer.
My contribution
Mechanical design of Stations 1 and 2 and the vacuum positioning jigs that transfer the ladder between them, checked by tolerance stack-up and ANSYS FEA. MSc thesis work, continued as a research fellow.
Outcome
A continuous datum-transfer design that, in analysis, meets both the positioning allowance and the panel-flatness limit.
Status
Manufactured and used for Layer 0 integration in the clean room. Positioning accuracy comes from analysis; the documented work includes no measured positioning result.

Update, October 2026 The new Layer-0 tracker has passed environmental testing and completed a proton-beam scan at CERN. Its installation on the ISS is planned for 2027.

Engineering detail ContextDatum-transfer architectureAnalysis and testing

Context

AMS-02 is a particle detector on the International Space Station. Its Layer 0 upgrade adds 768 silicon sensors, built into 72 ladders and bonded onto carbon-composite (CFRP–honeycomb) panels. This tooling assembles that hardware on the ground; it does not fly.

Datum-transfer architecture

Stations 1 and 2 work as one datum chain, not two separate fixtures. Station 1 aligns the ladder against fixed references, captures it on a vacuum positioning jig with no clamping on the sensors, and lays adhesive through a mask. The jig then carries the ladder’s reference to Station 2.

Station 2 is a 1100 × 1200 mm bonding fixture. The panel goes down on two guide pins running in 3 mm diagonal slots and slides 45° into place, so its bottom skin hooks under the caps of the support buttons, which set its height, hold it in z and limit sag; a pin in a hole and one in a slot then fix it in plane without over-constraining it. The relative heights of jig, ladder and panel set the bond-line thickness while the adhesive cures. Spherical-ended locating pins make placing a metre-long jig by hand forgiving, and three jig lengths share one interface.

Analysis and testing

A tolerance stack-up of the pin, hole and slot fits, machining tolerances and panel rotation gives a 93 µm worst-case ladder displacement. ANSYS FEA showed the initial 9-button support let the panel sag 62 µm, beyond the 50 µm limit; 13 buttons brought it to 26 µm. I also ran adhesive shear tests (PEEK / 3M 2216).

04Noove, Project Picoballoon2025–26

FD-11 radiosonde enclosure

A 6 g, 0.6 mm thin-wall SLA housing for an ultralight balloon-borne radiosonde.

Problem
Payload mass limits how high a small balloon flies, so the shell had an 8 g budget. It also carries the whole flight train, with the balloon above and the probe below, and must let the sensor board sit in free air, away from the electronics’ heat.
My contribution
Sole mechanical engineer: CAD, prototyping, assembly, testing, drawings and production release to an outside supplier (JLC3DP).
Outcome
Met the mass budget with margin, went into outsourced production, and has flown repeatedly to 15 km and −61 °C.
Status
Design frozen and in production. Clip behaviour under load in the cold and the 50-day mission duration are not yet validated.

Engineering detail Design and iterationFailure and fixTesting and its limits

Design and iteration

The shell is two 0.6 mm halves closed by four 0.4 mm snap-fit clips. Every opening in so thin a wall leaves a weak bridge, so local ribs restore thickness there without stiffening the surrounding wall. With cheap, fast prints and no FEA, I developed the clips empirically over seven iterations: three design changes, three to follow the probe’s changing interfaces, and one after a failure in flight.

Failure and fix

The tether ring broke once in flight, and the probe separated from the balloon and fell. The enclosure is the structural link in the flight train, so I thickened the ring; there has been no recurrence on later flights.

Testing and its limits

Validation included 1.5 m drops onto concrete (10 assembled units, five drops each) and a 30-minute −78 °C dry-ice soak with visual inspection, and every flight loads the shell and ring in service. The soak was static, so clip engagement and impact resistance at flight temperature remain untested. No flight has reached the 50-day mission target: flights ended when radio coverage was lost, and the longest verified flight is about 3 days.

The snap-fit is the first thing I would redesign: it took the most iterations and tolerates only a few open/close cycles.
